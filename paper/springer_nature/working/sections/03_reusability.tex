\section*{Reusability}

\subsection*{Five cancer cohorts and 400 frozen test sets}

We combined breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC) and lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) from TCGA and CPTAC, 3,238 patients in total (Fig.~\ref{fig:roadmap}b and Table~\ref{tab:cohorts}).\citep{tcga2012brca,tcga2017esca,tcga2015hnsc,tcga2012lusc,tcga2014luad,liu2018clinical,li2023pancancer} The original TCGA marker papers profiled fewer tumours than these cohorts contain (825 breast, 164 oesophageal, 279 head and neck, 178 squamous lung and 230 lung adenocarcinomas),\citep{tcga2012brca,tcga2017esca,tcga2015hnsc,tcga2012lusc,tcga2014luad} so our counts reflect the later PanCancer Atlas and CPTAC data and not those publications. The cohorts are also internally heterogeneous: the oesophageal cohort spans histological subtypes and the head and neck cohort spans HPV-positive and HPV-negative tumours from different anatomical sites.\citep{tcga2017esca,tcga2015hnsc} Patients censored before a horizon cannot be labeled, which left 1,645 patients for 3-year survival, 1,168 for 5-year survival and 937 for an exploratory extreme-survival endpoint that compares death within 3 years with survival beyond 5 years (Fig.~\ref{fig:roadmap}c).

This defines 16 prediction tasks: 13 cancer-specific tasks and three pooled endpoints. Each was split into five repeats of five patient-grouped folds, and the resulting 400 test sets were frozen before any model was run. In the pooled 3-year task, each model was fitted on about 1,050 patients and tested on about 330 per split (Fig.~\ref{fig:roadmap}d). Cancer-specific test sets were much smaller, usually fewer than 70 patients.

\subsection*{Within a single cancer, no model wins decisively}

Modeled one cancer at a time, survival was hard for every method (Fig.~\ref{fig:within}a). A foundation model had the highest mean ROC AUC in 7 of the 13 tasks, sometimes by a useful margin: in breast cancer extreme survival, TabPFN v2.6 reached 0.613 against 0.569 for the best classical model. Where foundation models trailed, they trailed by at most 0.024. Fold-matched differences from random forest were centered near zero for every nonlinear model (Fig.~\ref{fig:within}b).

Ranked across the 13 tasks, TabPFN v2.5 had the best mean rank (4.8), followed by TabPFN v2 (5.1) and random forest (5.2; Fig.~\ref{fig:within}c). The models differed overall (Friedman $\chi^2 = 20.3$, $P = 0.026$), but the difference came from logistic regression (mean rank 9.4). No two of the ten nonlinear models differed by more than the Nemenyi critical difference of 4.19 ranks.\citep{demsar2006statistical} In this setting TabPFN is reusable: it installs and runs without tuning and performed comparably to default-setting tree ensembles. A non-significant difference is not evidence of equivalence, and with cancer-specific test sets of usually fewer than 70 patients these comparisons could resolve only large differences. TabPFN is not, however, a clear improvement over the tree ensembles. Scored by Harrell C-index among eligible patients, within-cancer discrimination was about as low (0.50 to 0.54), and the ordering of models agreed with that by ROC AUC (Spearman 0.83 at 3 years, 0.67 at 5 years and 0.85 for extreme survival; Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed_cindex}). Averaged over endpoints, the within-cancer ROC AUC was 0.568 for TabPFN v2.5, 0.565 for TabFM and 0.561 for random forest (Table~\ref{tab:model_auc}).

\subsection*{Pooling lifts every capable model}

Training on all cancers together changed the picture abruptly (Fig.~\ref{fig:pooling}a). For 3-year survival, TabFM reached a mean ROC AUC of 0.721 and TabPFN v3 0.714, while random forest reached 0.716 and CatBoost 0.713. For 5-year survival, TabPFN v3 reached 0.754 against 0.749 for CatBoost, and for extreme survival 0.786 against 0.782. Every nonlinear model gained between 0.14 and 0.22 over its within-cancer level. Logistic regression, restricted to a linear function of the selected features, did not (0.536 at 3 years). The C-index shows the same pattern: over all pairs of patients in the pooled test sets it was 0.64 to 0.67 for the nonlinear models, but only 0.51 to 0.55 when pairs were restricted to patients with the same cancer (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed_cindex}).

Taken at face value this is the result that motivates pan-cancer modeling. Two features of it argue for caution. The gain was the same size for in-context foundation models, boosted trees and AutoML, more than would be expected from a capacity only some architectures have. And the largest values occurred for extreme survival, which removes the patients with intermediate outcomes and is therefore an easier question rather than a better answer to the fixed-horizon one.

\subsection*{Controls without patient biology approach the pooled scores}

We then asked how much of the pooled performance remains when patient biology is removed (Fig.~\ref{fig:pooling}b and Table~\ref{tab:shortcuts}). These controls were computed on a single earlier split of the pooled cohorts (141 to 247 test patients), so they place the benchmark scores in context rather than match them. A model given only the patient's cancer type reached ROC AUC 0.707 for 3-year survival, 0.703 for 5-year survival and 0.809 for extreme survival. A model given only the pattern of zero-valued features reached 0.745, 0.704 and 0.824. That pattern mixes measured zeros with features that are structurally absent from a cohort. When survival labels were shuffled within each cancer, preserving each cancer's event rate but removing any link between a patient's profile and outcome, ROC AUC still averaged 0.667, 0.653 and 0.662.

The zero pattern is an almost perfect record of the cohort. From it alone, cancer type was identified with a balanced accuracy of 0.985, against 0.480 from molecular values and 0.20 by chance (Fig.~\ref{fig:pooling}c). In the opposite direction, a linear molecular model trained on four cancers and tested on the fifth reached a mean ROC AUC of 0.479, 0.508 and 0.518 across the three endpoints.

\subsection*{Pooled training did not improve ranking within a cancer}

A clinician does not rank a breast cancer patient against a lung cancer patient; the diagnosis is already known. The comparison that matters is how well a model ranks patients who share a cancer. We therefore scored every test set within each cancer, for models trained on that cancer alone and for models trained on the pooled cohort, and compared the two. Each test set was scored on its own, as in Table~\ref{tab:model_auc}, and intervals come from a patient bootstrap that uses the same resampled patients for both kinds of training (Methods).

Pooled training did not clearly change within-cancer discrimination for most models (Fig.~\ref{fig:effect}a). Across the 30 model--endpoint comparisons, changes in ROC AUC ranged from $-0.049$ to $+0.041$, and three intervals excluded zero: AutoGluon ($-0.036$) and TabPFN v2.6 ($-0.049$) for extreme survival, and logistic regression ($+0.041$, lower bound 0.000) at 3 years. One or two such exclusions are expected by chance among 30 intervals. At 5 years, for example, TabFM changed by $-0.016$ (95\% CI, $-0.046$ to $0.014$) and TabPFN v3 by $-0.012$ ($-0.042$ to $0.018$), while random forest changed by $+0.005$ ($-0.024$ to $0.034$) and CatBoost by $+0.013$ ($-0.017$ to $0.042$).

The change was more negative for foundation models than for tree ensembles only on extreme survival (Fig.~\ref{fig:effect}c): by 0.021 in ROC AUC (95\% CI, $-0.043$ to $0.003$) and by 0.022 in Harrell C-index among eligible patients ($-0.036$ to $-0.007$; Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed_cindex}). At 3 years the difference was $+0.007$ ($-0.015$ to $0.028$) and at 5 years $-0.002$ ($-0.025$ to $0.019$). Cancer by cancer, pooling lowered ROC AUC in 13 of 20 foundation-model comparisons and 6 of 16 tree-ensemble comparisons for extreme survival, against 15 of 20 and 10 of 16 at 5 years (Fig.~\ref{fig:effect}b). The evidence for a foundation-model-specific cost of pooling is therefore limited to one endpoint, and its ROC AUC interval touches zero.

Our first version of this analysis averaged each patient's predictions over the five repeats and computed one AUC over all patients of a cancer, which pools predictions made by different models in different folds. It gave much larger losses for foundation models (for example $-0.084$ for TabFM at 5 years), and scoring within each test set reduced them to $-0.016$ (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:ed_pool}E). Predictions pooled across folds are sensitive to between-fold shifts in score scale, and in-context models, whose predictions depend on the training rows supplied, may be especially prone to them; we did not test this.
