\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Background:} Traditional tabular machine learning relies heavily on gradient boosted decision trees (GBDTs), which demand intensive hyperparameter tuning and manual preprocessing for every new task. Tabular Foundation Models (TFMs) represent a paradigm shift by enabling instant, parameter-free, in-context prediction directly on raw tabular datasets.

\noindent\textbf{Objective:} To establish a human-centered, standardized evaluation comparing the evolution of Prior-Fitted Networks (TabPFN v1 to v3) against Google's semantically pretrained TabFM (default and ensemble modes) on AutoML classification and clinical cancer survival tasks.

\noindent\textbf{Methods:} We leverage the modular \texttt{ev-tabpfn} validation engine to analyze seven standard classification tasks and ten TCGA cancer survival event classification tasks (Breast, Esophageal, Head \& Neck, and Lung cancers). We study clinical risk prediction across three difficulty tiers: (1) individual per-cancer modeling, (2) pooled cross-cancer modeling without cancer type indicators, and (3) extreme survival contrast (early death vs. long-term survival). To evaluate low-data regimes, we apply a stratified 1,024-row training cap.

\noindent\textbf{Results:} On standard datasets, TabFM 1.0.0 JAX default achieves superior mean accuracy (0.845) and log-loss calibration (0.355), outperforming TabPFN v3 by +3.0\% accuracy. However, TabPFN v2.5 maintains a slight lead in patient ranking quality (ROC AUC: 0.871 vs. 0.866). On noisy TCGA molecular datasets, individual per-cancer survival modeling is highly sample-starved (mean ROC AUC $\approx$ 0.53 - 0.56). However, when pooling cohorts cross-cancer to expand sample size, the models extract a generalizable survival signal, boosting ROC AUC to 0.743 (TabPFN v2.5). Under extreme survival contrast (early death vs. long-term survival), clinical noise is minimized, pushing ROC AUC to 0.831.

\noindent\textbf{Conclusion:} Tabular foundation models provide immediate in-context predictions. TabFM offers highly calibrated classification probabilities, while TabPFN yields rapid GPU forward-passes and robust generalization on high-dimensional clinical genomics.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Structured tabular data represents the primary information medium in administrative databases, financial profiling, and clinical diagnostics.\citep{chen2016xgboost} For decades, gradient boosted decision trees (GBDTs)—specifically XGBoost,\citep{chen2016xgboost} CatBoost,\citep{prokhorenkova2018catboost} and LightGBM\citep{ke2017lightgbm}—have held an empirical monopoly on tabular tasks. However, this success comes at a cost: practitioners must dedicate substantial time and compute to hyperparameter optimization (HPO), data imputation, and feature engineering for every new dataset.\citep{erickson2020autogluon} In clinical settings, where rapid, robust decision support is paramount, this tuning latency is a significant blocker to deployment.

Tabular Foundation Models (TFMs) aim to alleviate this practitioner's burden by executing zero-shot or in-context learning (ICL).\citep{hollmann2025accurate} By freezing the model's weights and passing a training "bag" (historical samples) along with a query point, TFMs generate instant predictions in a single forward pass. Two distinct philosophies have emerged to train these models:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Prior-Fitted Networks (PFNs)}: Exemplified by TabPFN,\citep{hollmann2025accurate} this architecture is pretrained entirely on millions of synthetically generated structural priors, learning to approximate Bayesian inference directly inside a transformer's attention layers.
  \item \textbf{Semantic Pretraining}: Exemplified by Google's TabFM,\citep{google2024tabfm} this architecture treats tables as tokenized text sequences. By pretraining on a massive corpus of real-world tables, TabFM learns deep semantic representations of columns and cellular entries, mapping tabular structures to target variables.
\end{itemize}

Evaluating these foundation models against GBDTs and Auto-ML platforms like AutoGluon\citep{erickson2020autogluon} is essential to establish their translation value. We developed the \texttt{ev-tabpfn} evaluation package to benchmark these systems under seed-consistent, uniform splits. This study evaluates the evolution of TabPFN weights (v1 to v3), Google's TabFM default, and a custom light TabFM ensemble ($n=4$) across standard benchmarks and clinical cancer cohorts.

\section{Dataset Curation \& Human Context}
\label{sec:datasets}

Rather than treating benchmark datasets as abstract matrices, we anchor them in their real-world applications and operational environments.

\subsection{AutoML Benchmark Profiles}
The standard evaluation suite consists of seven datasets that represent real-world screening and decision-making problems:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{ada}: A high-dimensional marketing dataset containing 4,147 samples and 48 features, representing customer response prediction.
  \item \textbf{australian}: A credit-card application screening task containing 690 samples and 14 features.
  \item \textbf{blood\_transfusion}: A compact clinical screening dataset (748 samples, 4 features) predicting donor recurrence at a blood transfusion center.
  \item \textbf{car}: A decision-support classification task (1,728 samples, 6 features) mapping vehicle attributes to consumer acceptance.
  \item \textbf{chum}: A customer retention dataset (5,000 samples, 20 features) predicting user churn.
  \item \textbf{cmc}: A demographic task (1,473 samples, 9 features) predicting contraceptive method choices based on socio-economic variables.
  \item \textbf{credit-g}: A credit-risk profiling dataset containing 1,000 samples and 20 features.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Deployment Hazards in Data Formats}
A critical finding from our data audits was that raw tabular target encodings are frequently inconsistent, presenting significant deployment hazards. For example:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,leftmargin=*]
  \item In the \textbf{blood\_transfusion} dataset, the binary target is encoded as $\{1, 2\}$ instead of $\{0, 1\}$.
  \item In the \textbf{credit-g} dataset, the target is encoded as strings (\texttt{bad} and \texttt{good}).
\end{itemize}
If an algorithm is deployed in a clinical or financial system with these raw formats, standard evaluation and visualization functions (e.g., ROC/AUC curves) will either crash or, worse, invert the prediction matrices—treating high-risk events as low-risk and vice versa. Our pipeline implements a standardized target normalization layer that maps binary categories to $\{0, 1\}$ and aligns multiclass strings alphabetically, acting as a safety barrier for deployment.

\subsection{The Low-Data Regime Simulation}

We partition each cohort case-wise into 60\% training (capped at 1,024), 20\% validation, and 20\% test splits using iterative multi-label stratification to preserve class distributions. \section{The ev-tabpfn Evaluation Engine}
\label{sec:methodology}

We developed the \texttt{ev-tabpfn} package to implement a reproducible, script-first evaluation stack. 

\subsection{Pipeline Architecture}
The framework is split into four distinct, automated phases:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Phase 1: Ingestion}: Loads local CSV files, auto-detects column delimiters, infers target columns, and performs stratified multi-label train-test splits.
  \item \textbf{Phase 2: Execution}: Integrates model adapters (XGBoost, CatBoost, AutoGluon, TabPFN, TabFM). Predictions are preserved at the sample level to allow offline metric recalculation.
  \item \textbf{Phase 3: Visualization}: Generates aggregate metrics, ROC curves, and radar charts.
  \item \textbf{Phase 4: Orchestration}: Batch execution command that iterates over datasets using a JSON configuration, incorporating failure-recovery (resume-friendly runs).
\end{itemize}

Google's TabFM is integrated as an adapter backend. The default model uses a single estimator with a JAX backend. To probe performance limits, we also implement a custom `tabfm-ensemble` preset using a reduced ensemble size ($n=4$) for CPU feasibility.

\subsection{TabPFN Generations Comparison}
Comparing the TabPFN models shows clear generational gains:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{v1} represents the weakest baseline, exhibiting the lowest accuracy (0.806) and the highest log loss (0.412).
  \item \textbf{v2} yields a substantial accuracy gain (0.817) and reduces log loss to 0.385.
  \item \textbf{v2.5} achieves the best class ranking, leading in ROC AUC (0.871) and log loss (0.383) among all TabPFN versions.
  \item \textbf{v3} shows balanced performance (Accuracy 0.815, ROC AUC 0.868) but does not outperform v2.5 on this specific benchmark.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{TabFM vs. TabPFN Comparison}
Google's TabFM 1.0.0 JAX demonstrates superior metric performance on tabular classification:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Accuracy Uplift}: TabFM achieves a mean accuracy of \textbf{0.845}, representing a +2.8\% improvement over the best TabPFN model (v2) and a +3.0\% improvement over TabPFN v3.
  \item \textbf{Log Loss Calibration}: TabFM reduces log loss to \textbf{0.355} (a -0.028 reduction over TabPFN v2.5), suggesting that its predicted probabilities are highly calibrated.
  \item \textbf{ROC AUC Ranking}: TabPFN v2.5 maintains a slight lead in ROC AUC (\textbf{0.871} vs. 0.866). This indicates that TabPFN has a slightly better sorting capability, even if TabFM fits classification thresholds more accurately.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Ensemble vs. Single Estimator}
Evaluating the light TabFM ensemble ($n=4$) reveals interesting behaviors:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,leftmargin=*]
  \item The ensemble achieves an ROC AUC of \textbf{0.867}, improving upon single-estimator TabFM (0.866) and approaching TabPFN v3.
  \item However, F1-score (0.738) and Accuracy (0.843) are slightly lower than the default JAX run. This indicates that a smaller ensemble does not reproduce the full scale-up gains observed in massive clusters, while substantially increasing prediction latency.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Inference Speed \& Computational Trade-offs}
Figure~\ref{fig:dashboard}F shows the fit and predict times. TabPFN models execute inference in milliseconds (sub-second prediction on GPU forward-passes). In contrast, because CUDA-enabled \texttt{jaxlib} was not installed, TabFM fell back to CPU execution. Single estimator TabFM requires $\approx$48s to fit and $\approx$27s to predict, which scales to $\approx$192s fit and $\approx$110s predict for the ensemble ($n=4$). This highlights a severe speed-to-accuracy trade-off for CPU deployments.

\section{Clinical Survival Event Classification Tasks}
\label{sec:survival}

To evaluate tabular foundation models on complex, noisy biomedical challenges, we benchmarked them on patient overall survival (OS) datasets from five TCGA cohorts: Breast Invasive Carcinoma (BRCA), Esophageal Carcinoma (ESCA), Head and Neck Squamous Cell Carcinoma (HNSCC), Lung Squamous Cell Carcinoma (LSCC), and Lung Adenocarcinoma (LUAD). 

\subsection{Clinical Target Formulation \& Exclusions}
In oncology clinical trials, identifying patients who are at risk of rapid disease progression is essential for early clinical intervention. We formulate this clinical question as a binary classification task under two fixed horizons: 3-year (1,095 days) and 5-year (1,825 days) overall survival events.

Using the clinical fields \texttt{OS\_days} (days followed until death or last contact) and \texttt{OS\_event} (vital status, where 1 indicates observed death and 0 indicates censored/alive), we define target labels as:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Class 1 (Event Observed)}: The patient passed away within the horizon ($OS\_event = 1$ and $OS\_days \le Horizon$).
  \item \textbf{Class 0 (Survived Window)}: The patient survived past the horizon ($OS\_days \ge Horizon$).
  \item \textbf{Excluded (Ambiguous)}: Patients followed for less than the horizon and censored alive ($OS\_event = 0$ and $OS\_days < Horizon$) are mathematically ambiguous because their survival outcome at the horizon is unknown. Keeping them would introduce label corruption; thus, they are excluded from the cohorts.
\end{itemize}

To evaluate these models under three progressive levels of clinical difficulty and cohort scale, we design three experiments: (1) individual per-cancer modeling, (2) pooled cross-cancer modeling without cancer type indicators, and (3) extreme survival contrast (early death vs. long-term survival). For all experiments, we select the top non-clinical high-variance molecular features (RNA-seq and DNA methylation probes) as predictors. The cohort characteristics and survival curves are visualized in Figure~\ref{fig:lifelines}.

\subsection{Scientific Interpretations}
Our hierarchical experimental structure reveals clear clinical insights:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Experiment 1: The Sample-Starvation Limit}: When trained on individual cancer cohorts (e.g., $n=86$ for ESCA), the sample-to-feature ratio is extremely unfavorable. The models behave as weak classifiers, struggle to beat random guessing, and yield low mean ROC AUCs (TabPFN v2.5: 0.4909, TabFM: 0.5301). 
  \item \textbf{Experiment 2: Cross-Cancer Generalization}: By pooling patient cohorts across all five cancer types (yielding $n=1,645$ for 3-year OS) and **removing cancer-type features**, we force the models to identify shared molecular survival patterns. This data-scaling results in a massive performance boost, pushing the ROC AUC to **0.7427** (TabPFN v2.5) and **0.7334** (TabFM default). This confirms that tabular foundation models can generalize across heterogeneous cancers if given sufficient sample sizes.
  \item \textbf{Experiment 3: Extreme Phenotype Separation}: By filtering out intermediate patients and comparing only the extreme clinical phenotypes—patients who died quickly (within 3 years) versus those who survived long-term (beyond 5 years)—we minimize label noise. The classification task becomes substantially cleaner, and model ranking performance surges, reaching an ROC AUC of **0.8305** (TabPFN v2.5) and **0.8026** (TabFM default).
\end{enumerate}

These findings demonstrate that while individual cancer genomics studies are frequently sample-starved, tabular foundation models can extract powerful, generalizable pan-cancer survival signals when patient cohorts are pooled or clinical outcomes are formulated to contrast extreme phenotypes.

\section{Discussion \& Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Our systematic study reveals that tabular foundation models are viable alternatives to traditional tree-based ensembles. 

By analyzing the results, we conclude:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Domain Pretraining Advantage}: Google's TabFM, pretrained on massive corpora of real tables, achieves better accuracy and calibration (log loss) than TabPFN, which is pretrained on synthetic data.
  \item \textbf{Prior-Fitted Efficiency}: TabPFN's architecture remains highly compute-efficient, providing sub-second in-context prediction on GPUs without requiring text-tokenization overhead.
  \item \textbf{Label Sensitivities}: Evaluation pipelines must incorporate robust label normalization layers to prevent target inconsistencies from crashing visualizers or inverting prediction matrices.
\end{enumerate}

Future research will extend the \texttt{ev-tabpfn} framework to support single-target regression benchmarks and evaluate these foundation models on larger datasets without sample capping.
