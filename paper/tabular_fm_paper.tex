\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Background:} Tabular foundation models such as TabPFN and TabFM predict from a labeled table in a single forward pass, without task-specific training, and are strongest on the small tables that are typical of clinical research. Cancer multiomics is an obvious place to use them, and an obvious place for them to be fooled: cohorts differ in prognosis, data source and which assays were measured, and all of this is visible in the feature table.

\noindent\textbf{Methods:} We evaluated five classical learners (logistic regression, random forest, CatBoost, XGBoost, LightGBM), AutoGluon, TabFM, and four TabPFN generations (v2, v2.5, v2.6, v3) on 3,238 patients across five cancer cohorts (breast, esophageal, head and neck, lung squamous, and lung adenocarcinoma) from TCGA and CPTAC. Outcomes were 3-year and 5-year overall-survival event classification and an exploratory extreme-survival contrast. Each task used five repeats of five-fold evaluation on 400 frozen test sets, validation-only threshold selection, and molecular feature selection confined to the training partition. We compared models trained within single cancers with models trained on randomly pooled cancers, and tested the pooled result against cancer-identity, missing-data, label-permutation and cancer-held-out controls.

\noindent\textbf{Results:} Under pan-cancer pooling, the best tabular foundation models reached a mean ROC AUC of 0.721 for 3-year survival ($n = 1{,}645$ patients) and 0.786 for extreme survival ($n = 937$), matched by random forest (0.716) and CatBoost (0.782). Within individual cancers, TabFM averaged only 0.565. A model given only the patient's cancer type reached 0.707 for 3-year survival, and one given only the pattern of missing measurements reached 0.745. Scored inside each cancer, pooled training lowered the ROC AUC of TabPFN v3 by 0.070 (95\% CI, 0.026--0.113) and of TabFM by 0.084 (0.037--0.130) at 5 years, while tree ensembles were unaffected. On an unseen, held-out cancer, discrimination fell to chance (mean ROC AUC 0.479).

\noindent\textbf{Conclusions:} The pooled gain is largely the cohort, not the patient. Foundation models were competitive with tree ensembles within cancers, but under pooling they absorbed cohort structure more readily and became worse at ranking patients who share a diagnosis. Pooled biomedical benchmarks should report within-cohort scores and shortcut controls alongside the headline number.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

For most of the history of machine learning on tables, the honest advice has been to use gradient-boosted trees. Random forests, XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost and AutoML systems built on them handle mixed feature scales, missing values and nonlinear interactions with little tuning, and careful benchmarks keep finding that deep networks struggle to beat them on medium-sized tabular data.\citep{breiman2001random,chen2016xgboost,ke2017lightgbm,prokhorenkova2018catboost,erickson2020autogluon,grinsztajn2022tree}

Tabular foundation models challenge that advice from an unusual direction. Rather than fitting parameters to each new dataset, a prior-fitted network such as TabPFN is pretrained once on millions of synthetic tables and then makes predictions by reading the labeled training rows as context.\citep{hollmann2025accurate} TabFM follows the same in-context principle with a different architecture and a synthetic prior built from structural causal models,\citep{google2026tabfm,tabfmsoftware2026} and TabICL extends the idea to larger tables.\citep{qu2025tabicl} The reported advantage is largest where data are scarce, which is precisely the regime of clinical research. A cancer cohort with a few hundred patients and thousands of molecular measurements looks, at first sight, like the ideal use case.

It also looks like an ideal trap. Cancer cohorts differ in baseline prognosis, in the institutions and consortia that produced them, and in which assays were run. When several cohorts are harmonized into one table, these differences do not disappear; they become features. A model can learn to recognize the cohort from its molecular profile, or from which columns are empty, and then predict survival from the cohort's average outcome. Such a model scores well under random cross-validation and transfers poorly, a failure that has been documented for batch effects and cross-study validation in genomics long before foundation models existed.\citep{soneson2014batch,bernau2014crossstudy,ambroise2002selection}

We came to this problem from the optimistic side. In early single-split experiments with TabPFN on five TCGA and CPTAC cohorts, pooling cancers lifted survival ROC AUC from roughly 0.55 to above 0.74, and we initially read this as a shared pan-cancer prognostic signal. When we rebuilt the analysis with leakage-safe splits, the lift remained, but so did a simpler explanation: the patient's cancer type alone predicted survival almost as well. This paper is the result of taking that explanation seriously.

We ask four questions on identical, frozen patient splits. How do four TabPFN generations, TabFM, AutoGluon and five classical learners compare when each cancer is modeled on its own? How much does pooling raise discrimination? How much of that rise can be reproduced without any molecular biology, from cancer identity or the pattern of missing data? And when the pooled models are scored only among patients who share a diagnosis, the comparison that matters to a clinician, does pooling help or hurt? We frame survival as fixed-horizon event classification, which allows identical metrics across all eleven models, and return to censored time-to-event modeling in the Discussion.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Five cohorts, three survival targets and 400 frozen test sets}

The study combined breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC) and lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) from TCGA and CPTAC, a total of 3,238 patients (Figure~\ref{fig:cohort_design}).\citep{tcga2012brca,tcga2017esca,tcga2015hnsc,tcga2012lusc,tcga2014luad,liu2018clinical,li2023pancancer} Cohorts ranged from 182 patients (ESCA) to 1,206 (BRCA). A fixed-horizon label requires knowing a patient's status at the horizon, so patients censored earlier were excluded. This left 1,645 patients for 3-year survival, 1,168 for 5-year survival and 937 for the extreme contrast between death within 3 years and survival beyond 5 years. ESCA was too small to form the two longer endpoints (Table~\ref{tab:cohorts}).

These choices define 16 prediction tasks: 13 cancer-specific tasks and the three endpoints in the pooled cohort. Each task was split into five repeats of five patient-grouped folds, giving 400 test sets that were frozen before any model was run, so that every model saw exactly the same patients. In the pooled 3-year task, for example, each model was fitted on about 1,050 patients and evaluated on about 330 held-out patients per split, and every one of the 1,645 patients was scored out of fold. Cancer-specific test sets were much smaller, usually fewer than 70 patients, which matters for how their differences should be read.

\subsection{Within a single cancer, every model finds the problem hard}

Modeled one cancer at a time, survival was difficult for every method (Figure~\ref{fig:within_cancer}). Averaged over the cancer-specific tasks, the foundation models reached a mean ROC AUC of 0.565 (TabFM), with the best, TabPFN v2.5, at 0.568. Random forest reached 0.561, CatBoost 0.559 and AutoGluon 0.550 (Table~\ref{tab:model_auc}). A foundation model had the highest mean ROC AUC in 7 of the 13 tasks. Where they led, the margin was sometimes meaningful: in breast cancer extreme survival, TabPFN v2.6 reached 0.613 against 0.569 for the best classical model, and in lung squamous 5-year survival TabPFN v3 reached 0.606 against 0.571. Where they trailed, they trailed by at most 0.024.

We read this as a modest, real but fragile advantage, not a victory. With test sets of usually fewer than 70 patients, fold-to-fold variation was larger than most differences between models, and fold-matched differences from random forest were centered close to zero (Figure~\ref{fig:within_cancer}D). Precision-recall performance largely tracked event prevalence, which differed widely across tasks, so we report it relative to the prevalence baseline (Figure~\ref{fig:within_cancer}E).

\subsection{Pooling cancers lifts every capable model}

Training on all cancers together changed the picture abruptly (Figure~\ref{fig:pooling}). For 3-year survival, TabFM reached a mean ROC AUC of 0.721 and TabPFN v3 0.714, but random forest reached 0.716 and CatBoost 0.713. For 5-year survival, TabPFN v3 reached 0.754 against 0.749 for CatBoost. For the extreme contrast, TabPFN v3 reached 0.786 against 0.782 for CatBoost. Every nonlinear model rose by 0.15 to 0.20 over its within-cancer level, and the ROC curves of the five fully evaluated models were nearly indistinguishable (Figure~\ref{fig:pooling}B). Only logistic regression, restricted to a linear function of the selected features, failed to benefit (0.536 at 3 years).

Taken at face value, this is the result that motivates pan-cancer modeling: more patients, more signal, better prediction. Two features of it should give pause. First, the gain was the same size for in-context foundation models, boosted trees and AutoML, which is more than one would expect if it came from a capacity that only some architectures possess. Second, the largest values appeared in the extreme contrast, which removes patients with intermediate outcomes and is therefore an easier and different question rather than a better answer to the fixed-horizon one.

\subsection{Most of the pooled gain can be reproduced without molecular biology}

We then asked how much of the pooled performance survives when the biology is taken away (Figure~\ref{fig:shortcuts}, Table~\ref{tab:shortcuts}). A model given nothing but the patient's cancer type reached a mean ROC AUC of 0.707 for 3-year survival, 0.703 for 5-year survival and 0.809 for the extreme contrast. A model given only the pattern of which measurements were present or absent, without their values, reached 0.745, 0.704 and 0.824. For 3-year survival and the extreme contrast, the missing-data pattern alone outperformed every molecular model we evaluated.

The reason is that the missing-data pattern is a near-perfect fingerprint of the cohort. Harmonizing five cohorts with different assay coverage produces blocks of structurally empty features, and from those blocks alone the cancer type could be identified with a balanced accuracy of 0.985, where chance is 0.20. Label permutation gave the same message from another angle. When survival labels were shuffled across all patients, pooled ROC AUC fell to about 0.50, as it should. When they were shuffled only within each cancer, preserving each cancer's event rate but destroying every link between a patient's profile and their outcome, pooled ROC AUC remained 0.667 at 3 years and 0.653 at 5 years. About three-quarters of the pooled models' gain over chance is therefore available to a model that knows nothing about individual patients.

The cancer-held-out test asked the complementary question: does what is learned from four cancers transfer to a fifth? A linear molecular model trained on the remaining cancers reached a mean ROC AUC of 0.479 for 3-year survival on the held-out cancer, 0.508 for 5-year survival and 0.518 for the extreme contrast, all indistinguishable from chance.

\subsection{Pooled training made foundation models worse at ranking patients with the same cancer}

A clinician never needs to rank a breast cancer patient against a lung cancer patient; the diagnosis is known. The question that matters is whether a model ranks patients within the same disease correctly. Because the cancer-specific and pooled analyses scored the same patients, we could compare them directly: for each patient, the prediction from a model trained on their cancer alone against the prediction from a model trained on the pooled cohort, both scored only within that cancer (Figure~\ref{fig:pooling}E).

Pooling did not significantly improve within-cancer ranking for any foundation model, and for several it made it worse. At 5 years, pooled training lowered the within-cancer ROC AUC of TabPFN v3 from 0.539 to 0.469, a difference of $-0.070$ (95\% CI, $-0.113$ to $-0.026$), and of TabFM from 0.540 to 0.456 ($-0.084$; $-0.130$ to $-0.037$), placing both below chance. For the extreme contrast, TabFM fell by 0.081 ($-0.127$ to $-0.035$) and TabPFN v2.6 by 0.068 ($-0.113$ to $-0.020$). Tree ensembles behaved differently: random forest, CatBoost, XGBoost and LightGBM changed by at most 0.04 at any endpoint, with every confidence interval spanning zero. The only models that gained were the weakest within-cancer performers at 3 years, logistic regression ($+0.071$) and AutoGluon ($+0.073$).

In other words, the same pooling step that raised overall ROC AUC by 0.15 to 0.20 left the trees' within-cancer ranking unchanged and degraded that of the foundation models. The pooled models were not learning more about patients; the foundation models, in particular, appear to have traded patient-level information for cohort-level information.

\subsection{Probability quality and cost}

Discrimination is not the whole of a prediction. In the pooled tasks, the foundation models produced slightly better probabilities than the trees, with log loss of 0.514 to 0.518 for the extreme contrast against 0.524 for CatBoost and 0.544 for random forest, and Brier scores that were similar or marginally lower (Figure~\ref{fig:probability_compute}). Calibration curves nonetheless showed deviations that ROC AUC does not reveal, and sensitivity and specificity depended on thresholds chosen on validation data, so we treat them as secondary.

The models differed far more in cost than in accuracy. On the same CPU hardware, the tree ensembles fitted each fold in a few seconds or less and predicted almost instantly, whereas the foundation models, which defer most computation to prediction time, took 11 to 38 seconds per fold to predict. AutoGluon was run with a fixed 180-second search budget per fold.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

We set out to ask whether tabular foundation models can predict cancer survival from multiomics better than the methods they are meant to replace. Within a single cancer, the answer is a qualified yes: they were the best model in a little over half of the tasks, sometimes by a useful margin, but never decisively enough to separate them from random forest or CatBoost given the size of the test sets. Across cancers, the answer depends on what one means by better. Pooling produced numbers that would be striking in an abstract, and nearly all of that improvement could be reproduced by a model that knows only which cancer a patient has, or which assays were run on them.

The result we did not expect is the one in Figure~\ref{fig:pooling}E. Pooling did not merely fail to help within-cancer prediction; it made the foundation models worse at it, while leaving tree ensembles essentially untouched. We do not have a mechanistic account of this difference, and we think it deserves one. Our working hypothesis is that in-context prediction rewards whatever structure most cleanly separates the context rows. In the pooled table, the cleanest structure is the cohort, which is recoverable almost perfectly from the missing-data pattern, and a model that conditions on similar training patients will find that the most similar patients are those with the same cancer and, on average, the same outcome. Tree ensembles, which split greedily on individual features and average over many random subspaces, may be less able to collapse onto a single latent variable in this way. The hypothesis is testable, for example by removing the missing-data fingerprint or standardizing features within each cancer before pooling, and we would be glad to see it refuted.

There is a reasonable counterargument to our reading. If a model is to be deployed on an unselected pan-cancer population, cohort membership is part of the prediction problem, and a model that exploits it is not wrong. We disagree for this setting, for a practical reason: in oncology the cancer type is known before any molecular profile is taken, so a survival model earns its place only by ranking patients within a diagnosis. A pooled ROC AUC of 0.72 answers a question no clinician asks, while the within-cancer ROC AUC of the same models, close to or below 0.5, answers the one they do. The disagreement is worth having openly, because the pooled number is the one that tends to appear in abstracts.

The extreme-survival contrast illustrates a related point about study design. Comparing early deaths with long-term survivors produced the highest scores in the study, but it does so by excluding the patients whose outcomes are hardest to predict. It is useful for exploring biology and inappropriate as evidence of predictive performance, and its stronger shortcut controls (0.809 for cancer type, 0.824 for missing data) show that cohort composition matters even more when outcomes are pushed apart.

Several limitations bound these conclusions. Fixed-horizon classification discards patients censored before the horizon, about half the cohort at 3 years, and does not use the timing of events. Cancer-specific test sets were small, so individual task-level differences are uncertain. The paired within-cancer analysis averages each patient's predictions over five repeats and bootstraps patients, so its intervals reflect sampling of patients rather than retraining variability. The cancer-held-out diagnostic used a linear model on features preselected in the pooled cohort; this choice can only flatter it, which strengthens rather than weakens its null result, but we did not repeat it with foundation models. Pooled 3-year results are missing for TabPFN v2 to v2.6 because of a CPU sample limit, and we did not impute them. Finally, all evaluation was internal to TCGA and CPTAC.

The natural next steps follow directly from these limitations: censored time-to-event modeling that retains nearly all 3,238 patients and is applied identically to every model; pooling strategies that standardize features within each cancer and remove the missing-data fingerprint, to test whether pooled context can ever help within-cancer ranking; a direct comparison of molecular features with clinical stage and age; and validation in an independent cohort. Until such evidence exists, we suggest a simple standard for biomedical evaluations of tabular foundation models: identical frozen patient splits, preprocessing confined to training data, within-cohort results reported next to pooled ones, cohort-only and missing-data-only controls, and at least one cohort-held-out test. Under that standard, a high pooled score is the beginning of an argument rather than its conclusion.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Data sources and cohort definition}

We used harmonized molecular and clinical data for BRCA, ESCA, HNSCC, LSCC and LUAD. TCGA PanCancer Atlas studies and the TCGA clinical data resource provided disease definitions and outcomes; CPTAC proteogenomic studies contributed additional patients for every cancer except ESCA.\citep{tcga2012brca,tcga2017esca,tcga2015hnsc,tcga2012lusc,tcga2014luad,liu2018clinical,li2023pancancer} Molecular modalities comprised RNA expression, copy number, DNA methylation and somatic mutation. Clinical covariates were excluded from all models. Patient identifiers were used as grouping units so that no patient's records could appear in more than one of the training, validation and test partitions.

\subsection{Outcome definitions}

For a horizon $h$, a patient was assigned class 1 when death was observed on or before $h$ and class 0 when follow-up extended to or beyond $h$. Patients censored before $h$ had unknown status and were excluded. Horizons were 1,095 days (3 years) and 1,825 days (5 years). The exploratory extreme-survival endpoint contrasted death within 3 years with survival beyond 5 years and was analyzed separately because it excludes intermediate outcomes and targets a different population.

\subsection{Repeated patient-grouped evaluation}

Each task used five repeats of five outer folds. In each fold, about 20\% of patients formed the test set; the remainder was divided into training (about 64\% of patients overall) and validation (about 16\%) partitions. Splits were patient-grouped and stratified by outcome where feasible, and all 400 test sets were frozen, with checksums, before any model was run. For pooled tasks, cohorts were harmonized on exact shared feature identifiers, with features absent from a cohort set to zero. Within each fold, the 100 highest-variance molecular features were selected on training patients only. Validation patients were used only to choose a classification threshold; test patients were never used for preprocessing, feature selection, model choice or threshold selection.

\subsection{Models}

We compared logistic regression, random forest, CatBoost, XGBoost, LightGBM, AutoGluon, TabFM, and TabPFN v2, v2.5, v2.6 and v3.\citep{breiman2001random,chen2016xgboost,ke2017lightgbm,prokhorenkova2018catboost,erickson2020autogluon,hollmann2025accurate,google2026tabfm,tabfmsoftware2026} Foundation models were used with their released default settings and no fine-tuning; TabFM used its PyTorch implementation. AutoGluon was given a fixed time limit of 180 seconds per fold. All models ran on CPU. TabPFN v2, v2.5 and v2.6 do not permit CPU inference with more than 1,000 training samples, so the pooled 3-year task (about 1,050 training patients per fold) could not be evaluated for those versions; these cells are reported as NA and were not imputed.

\subsection{Metrics}

For every model and test set we recorded ROC AUC, precision-recall AUC, log loss, Brier score, and accuracy, balanced accuracy, sensitivity, specificity and F1 score at the validation-selected threshold. ROC AUC was the primary measure. Precision-recall AUC was interpreted relative to event prevalence. Summary values are means across the 25 test sets of each task; within-cancer summaries are macro-averages across the cancer-specific tasks. Out-of-fold ROC, precision-recall and calibration curves were computed from patient-level predictions averaged across repeats.

\subsection{Shortcut and generalization controls}

The cancer-identity control was a logistic model whose only input was the cancer type. The missing-data control used a binary indicator of whether each harmonized feature was structurally absent, without measured values. Cohort identifiability was estimated by predicting cancer type from molecular values or from the missing-data pattern. Label-permutation nulls used 500 permutations per endpoint, either across all patients (global) or within each cancer, which preserves each cancer's event rate while removing any patient-level association. In the cancer-held-out diagnostic, a linear molecular model was trained on all but one cancer and evaluated on the omitted one; features for this diagnostic were preselected in the pooled cohort.

\subsection{Paired within-cancer comparison}

Every patient in a pooled task also appears in the corresponding cancer-specific task. For each model and endpoint, we averaged each patient's test-set probability across the five repeats separately for the cancer-specific and the pooled model, computed the ROC AUC of each within every cancer, and took the difference. Uncertainty was estimated with 2,000 patient bootstrap resamples, stratified by outcome class within each cancer; the summary for each model and endpoint is the mean difference across cancers, with a 95\% percentile interval from the same resamples.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

Fold-level metrics, patient-level predictions, selected-feature records, fold manifests and software metadata are retained in the project repository. All figures and tables were generated from these artifacts by \path{paper/analysis/build_manuscript_assets.py}, and the paired within-cancer comparison by \path{paper/analysis/within_cancer_pooling_contrast.py}; no curves or intervals were simulated or entered by hand. Reporting followed TRIPOD+AI, with risk of bias considered against PROBAST+AI.\citep{tripodai2024,probastai2024}
