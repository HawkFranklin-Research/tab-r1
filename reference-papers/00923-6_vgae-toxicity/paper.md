## Abstract

Variational graph encoders effectively combine graph convolutional networks with variational autoencoders, and have been widely employed for biomedical graph-structured data. Lam and colleagues developed a framework based on the variational graph encoder, NYAN, to facilitate the prediction of molecular properties in computer-assisted drug design. In NYAN, the low-dimensional latent variables derived from the variational graph autoencoder are leveraged as a universal molecular representation, yielding remarkable performance and versatility throughout the drug discovery process. In this study we assess the reusability of NYAN and investigate its applicability within the context of specific chemical toxicity prediction. The prediction accuracy—based on NYAN latent representations and other popular molecular feature representations—is benchmarked across a broad spectrum of toxicity datasets, and the adaptation of NYAN latent representation to other surrogate models is also explored. NYAN, equipped with common surrogate models, shows competitive or better performance in toxicity prediction compared with other state-of-the-art molecular property prediction methods. We also devise a multi-task learning strategy with feature enhancement and consensus inference by leveraging the low dimensionality and feature diversity of NYAN latent space, further boosting the multi-endpoint acute toxicity estimation. The analysis delves into the adaptability of the generic graph variational model, showcasing its aptitude for tailored tasks within the realm of drug discovery.

## Introduction

Computer-aided drug design (CADD) accelerates and optimizes the drug discovery process. Molecular properties prediction, which involves the identification of molecules with desired properties, is a crucial task in the CADD process[1,2]. Variational graph autoencoders (VGAEs) represent a cutting-edge deep generative algorithm, wherein the core principles of variational autoencoders have been adapted and expanded for graph representation learning. Variational graph autoencoders are capable of modelling complex graph structures within biomedical data, effectively addressing the challenges in AI-aided drug discovery[3,4]. Lam and colleagues introduced the NYAN framework, which incorporates a variational graph encoder, along with downstream surrogate models, into its architecture[5]. This model not only rivals the performance of current state-of-the-art techniques, but also operates at a faster speed while providing superior interpretability. It has the potential to redirect CADD towards computer-aided drug engineering.

In this report we assess the reproducibility and reusability of NYAN with a focus on its application in predictive toxicology (Fig. 1). Toxicity prediction, which involves ensuring the safety and efficacy of potential medications, is an essential stage in drug discovery. Reports indicate that more than 30% of drug candidates are withdrawn due to toxicity concerns[6,7]. To examine the model's reproducibility, we initially retrain it and make predictions using the toxicity datasets described in the original paper[5]. We then explore the impact of different molecular representations and downstream surrogate models on the prediction performance using a public large-scale toxicity database, TOXRIC, which was constructed and published in one of our past works[8]. We then compare NYAN latent representations equipped with surrogate models to other state-of-the-art sophisticated molecular property prediction methods based on transformer, graph neural networks (GNNs), long short-term memory (LSTM), knowledge graphs and so on. Finally, for the crucial task of multi-endpoint acute toxicity estimation in drug toxicity evaluation, we devise a multi-task learning strategy with feature enhancement and consensus inference; this strategy integrates NYAN latent representations with multi-task learning to achieve improved acute toxicity estimation.

## Reproducing the reported results

The original paper[5] used 650,000 different compound molecules from the ZINC database to pre-train its NYAN framework for a lengthy training duration. To reduce the pre-training time, we attempted to retrain the model using only half of the original paper's training data (325,000 molecules versus the original 650,000), while keeping other configurations consistent with those of the original paper. We then retrained and tested the NYAN surrogate models, that is, ExtraTree classifiers, to examine the reproducibility of NYAN on datasets associated with molecular absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity (ADMET) properties, as also mentioned in the original work. Concretely, we performed five-fold cross-validated ADMET classifications for blood–brain barrier (BBB) penetration, P-glycoprotein (Pgp) inhibition, CYP2D6 liver enzyme inhibition (Fig. 2a,b) and toxicology predictions across 56 Tox21 assay endpoints (Fig. 2c).

The results show that the reproduced results are aligned with the originals[5] in the general ADMET classification and toxicology prediction across different assays. The retrained model performed almost as well as the original on three datasets: BBB Martins, Pgp Broccatelli and CYP2D6 Veith. The BBB Martins and Pgp Broccatelli datasets measure the distribution properties of drugs. For the BBB Martins dataset, the original study outperformed the retrained model in the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) and accuracy metrics; however, for the Pgp Broccatelli dataset, the reproduced results surpassed the original in both the AUROC and accuracy metrics. The CYP2D6 Veith dataset examines the metabolic properties of compounds and, for this dataset, the reproduced results exceeded the original model in both the AUROC and AUPRC metrics. The Tox21 assay, which comprises 56 sub-datasets, evaluates the toxicity of compounds. The experimental results show that the retrained model achieved an AUROC of 0.865 ± 0.026, AUPRC of 0.988 ± 0.003 and accuracy of 0.951 ± 0.006, surpassing the original results of 0.856 ± 0.027, 0.982 ± 0.003 and 0.934 ± 0.005, respectively. Our reproduced results also exceeded those reported by the original study on most of the 56 Tox21 sub-datasets (Supplementary Fig. 1). Moreover, the statistical comparison with regard to the three metrics suggests that the reproduced values are consistent with the original results (Fig. 2d). In conclusion, the newly trained NYAN framework achieves original predictive performance, despite using fewer training data for the graph variational encoder, demonstrating the robustness of this framework. This experimental finding could also be beneficial for future researchers, as it can further reduce the pre-training time of the NYAN framework without compromising its performance.

## Benchmarking of different molecular representation methods

Optimized prediction of ADMET properties is crucial at different stages of drug development; however, the assessment of chemical toxicity remains a substantial challenge and is central to drug design. An ideal drug design strikes a balance between efficacy and toxicity. In fact, toxicity-induced safety issues are a primary reason for the withdrawal of new drugs during preclinical and clinical phases. Studies estimate that the success rate of clinical drug trials could increase by over 40% if drug toxicity could be mitigated. Artificial-intelligence-aided computational drug toxicity prediction has emerged as a more accurate and efficient method for identifying safety issues of new compounds before they enter human clinical trials, thus saving time and cost[9,10].

To further explore the efficacy and differences of different molecular feature representation in chemical toxicity prediction, we selected a broader spectrum of toxicity datasets and benchmarked the differences in toxicity prediction between molecular features generated by NYAN variational graph autoencoders and other typical molecular feature representations. Concretely, we obtained 30 toxicity datasets of binary classification tasks from the TOXRIC database and used the well-trained variational graph autoencoder NYAN to generate the molecular latent representations (entitled NYAN latent here); these were compared with nine types of molecular features, including Rdkit2D, Mordred, Avalon, Atom-pair, Morgan512, Morgan1024, Topological Torsion, MACCS and ECFP2. The ExtraTree classifier was uniformly adopted as the downstream model to accomplish the classification tasks for 30 toxicity datasets.

A performance comparison between different molecular representation methods is mainly conducted on the basis of their performance ranking on all 30 datasets. It was observed that the ranking distribution of NYAN latent is second only to RDKit2D (Fig. 3a); it is superior to most other molecular representation methods despite its low dimensionality. Furthermore, the difference in the molecular representation benchmarking was ranked using Nemenyi test in the critical difference diagram (Fig. 3b), which shows that RDKit2D is the best upstream feature representation for the unified surrogate model (that is, ExtraTree classifiers). The NYAN latent generated by the variational graph encoder performed second best. The numerical performance (Fig. 3c) achieved by NYAN latent is also second only to RDKit2D. Overall, NYAN latent as the molecular feature representation is in the top tier for toxicity prediction tasks, proving that NYAN's feature extraction has competitive effects on toxicity prediction.

## Exploration of competent surrogate models

We further explore the competence of surrogate models in toxicity prediction, under the same molecular representation extracted by the NYAN's variational graph encoder. Inspired by the use of ExtraTree as a surrogate model in the original work[5], which achieved satisfactory classification results, we further introduced an ensemble–ensemble architecture model, deep forest, to enhance the performance of downstream models (Fig. 4a). For a more systematic comparison, we evaluated popular contemporary toxicity classification algorithms such as ExtraTree, support vector machine (SVM), random forest, AdaBoost, light gradient-boosting machine, gradient-boosting decision tree and XGBoost, and assessed their compatibility and adaptability to NYAN latent representations.

The Friedman and Nemenyi tests were introduced to analyse the performance differences among the eight surrogate models. The results show that deep forest, as an ensemble–ensemble architecture, achieves superior performance in terms of numerical performance (Fig. 4b), ranking distribution (Fig. 4c) and average ranking (Fig. 4d), whereas ExtraTree has the second-highest performance, while SVM also shows relatively outstanding performance. These findings validated our hypothesis: the integration of multiple ExtraTrees within the deep forest can optimize the performance of the downstream surrogate model.

## Evaluation against state-of-the-art baselines

In advancing research within the field of computational toxicology, we conducted a comprehensive comparison of the NYAN latent representations equipped with surrogate models against state-of-the-art models across all 30 toxicity datasets. The evaluation encompassed an array of molecular property prediction methods, including transformer-based models (KPGT[11], UniMol[12] and PharmHGT[13]), a GNN-based model (MolCLR[14]), an LSTM-based model (BAN[15]) and a knowledge graph-based model (KANO[16]). These baselines may contain more complex network architectures, undergo extensive pre-training on large amounts of data or use molecular representations with richer modalities. The average AUROC, AUPRC and accuracy scores over five-fold cross-validation were used as the performance metrics.

Compared with these sophisticated state-of-the-art models, the low-dimensional and concise NYAN latent representation presents commendable adaptability to toxic datasets despite the use of common surrogate models, yielding notable prediction performance in specific toxicological applications (Fig. 5). Under the AUROC (Fig. 5a) and accuracy (Fig. 5c) metrics, the NYAN latent representation with ExtraTree or deep forest achieved a very competitive performance (and was only weaker than KPGT and UniMol). It should be emphasized that the NYAN model exhibits a clear advantage in the AUPRC metric compared with other state-of-the-art models (Fig. 5b). We attribute this to the imbalance between positive and negative samples in 30 toxicity datasets (Supplementary Table 1), and it is well-known that AUPRC is more sensitive to this imbalance than AUROC and accuracy. This observation highlights that, in addressing toxicity predictions, the latent representations extracted by NYAN—when integrated with some classic downstream surrogate models—proficiently accommodate variations in dataset size and tackle the problem of sample imbalance, showcasing strong robustness. Consequently, NYAN emerges as a versatile, dependable and trustworthy methodology for toxicity prediction.

## Adaptation for acute toxicity estimation

Acute toxicity estimation is a crucial aspect of chemical toxicity assessment; it requires reliable computational models for making accurate predictions, and also to support the global 3Rs initiative (replacement, reduction and refinement) to reduce animal testing[17,18]. However, acute toxicity estimation presents challenges due to its complexity and data imbalance with multiple different endpoints from various species, administration routes and assay records. Multi-task learning has proven effective in addressing these challenges[19]. Past research has shown that Avalon molecular fingerprints excel in multi-endpoint acute toxicity tasks[20]. On the basis of these findings, we aim to design a novel learning strategy to integrate NYAN latent representations with a multi-task learning paradigm and further improve the multi-endpoint acute toxicity estimation performance.

First, we obtained the acute toxicity dataset from TOXRIC database, which consists of 80,081 compounds and involves 59 different toxicity endpoints with a certain amount of toxicity measurements per endpoint. We then employed the retrained NYAN 325,000 model to extract the latent representations of the 80,081 compounds, subjecting them to five-fold cross-validation, and evaluating the toxicity regression performance on the basis of the determination coefficient (R²) and root-mean-squared error (RMSE) metrics across 59 endpoint tasks. Here we developed a multi-task learning strategy with feature enhancement and consensus inference by fully leveraging the low dimensionality and feature diversity of NYAN latent space. Specifically, the NYAN latent is concatenated with Avalon to form an enhanced molecular representation, and a multi-task deep neural network can be trained using these enhanced representations (Fig. 6a). Considering that NYAN is a variational autoencoder based generative framework and for arbitrary molecular SMILES the variational graph encoder can derive an infinite number of NYAN latent representations belonging to a specific Gaussian distribution in hidden space, we devised a consensus inference strategy which combines the predictions of five random NYAN latent representations for each test sample, in the hope of reaching a more accurate and robust toxicity predictive value. Concretely, the molecular fingerprint feature Avalon is concatenated with each NYAN latent representation, respectively. This operation will lead to five different enhanced representations (Fig. 6a). These enhanced representations will be separately fed into a multi-task deep neural network, leading in five groups of different acute toxicity predictive values. Their average predictive values will be treated as the final multi-endpoint acute toxicity estimation results for the compound molecule.

Compared with past cutting-edge acute toxicity prediction methods[20]—including single-task deep neural networks, single-task random forest, multi-task deep neural networks, graph convolutional neural networks and deep learning consensus architectures—the multi-task learning framework equipped with NYAN latent, termed MT-NYAN, is experimentally proven to further improve the estimation performance on the acute toxicity tasks. Specifically, MT-NYAN with five random latent representations achieved the best performance with regards to the averaged R² and RMSE over all 59 endpoints (Fig. 6b,c); furthermore, its performance ranks in the top two on most of the 59 endpoints (Fig. 6d). With regards to endpoints, MT-NYAN surpassed other baseline methods on most of the toxicity endpoints (Fig. 6e)—especially on those of the upper half, which have relatively limited amounts of toxicity measurement data available for training. Another important observation is that the consensus inference strategy utilizing five random NYAN latent representations can yield more balanced improvements over 59 endpoints compared with MT-NYAN with a single NYAN latent. Furthermore, their difference is proven to be statistically significant (Fig. 6f,g), indicating the effectiveness of the latent space learned by NYAN, as well as the complementarity between the random representations distributed in the latent space. Furthermore, we selected one of the five-fold cross-validation experiments and applied a well-trained MT-NYAN (five random latents) to the test fold to extract the top-level embeddings for the testing compounds, which were then visualized using t-SNE (Fig. 6h). The visualization results present a well-defined feature manifold regarding toxicity intensity for various toxicity endpoints, implying that NYAN latent representation can assist in multi-task learning model to learn better molecular embeddings for acute toxicity estimation. This study could inspire researchers to better use the diversity and randomness of the latent representations extracted by NYAN framework to enhance the robustness of downstream models.

## Discussion and future directions

This reusability report replicates the original work's predictive results[5] on general molecular properties. Using a broader range of toxicity data, we validated the NYAN framework proposed in the original work for its capability in toxicity assessment. We found that the graph variational autoencoder from the original study has a notable advantage in molecular feature representation. We also explored the performance of downstream models beyond the surrogate model proposed in the original work and discovered that the ensemble-learning method, deep forest, can further enhance the performance of NYAN latent representation in molecular toxicity prediction. Experimental comparison with currently advanced molecular property prediction methods based on transformer, GNN, LSTM and knowledge graph further demonstrated the superiority of combining NYAN latent representations with concise surrogate models for toxicity prediction. Furthermore, we designed a multi-task learning strategy with feature enhancement and consensus inference by fully leveraging the low dimensionality and feature diversity of NYAN latent space, further improving the performance of multi-endpoint acute toxicity estimation. Overall, regarding predictive toxicology, we discussed the versatility of the original model in detail, concluding that the NYAN framework is not only a generalist but also a specialist.

Although the NYAN latent representation has been proven to be efficient and concise with good universality, there is still a possibility of optimizing the structure of variational graph encoders. For example, its training currently only relies on the molecular SMILES strings with graph-structured information between atoms. Incorporating richer and more detailed molecular information to further improve the representation learning ability of variational graph encoders, such as the 3D structural knowledge, multimodal molecular data, and so on, will be a potential improvement direction in the future.

## Methods

### Reproducibility of the original results

We conducted the reproduction experiments using the public code of NYAN from its GitHub repository[21]. The code package encompasses all of the environment configurations, instructions and operational examples necessary for ensuring reproducibility. The molecular data to retrain the NYAN framework were traced from the ZINC database[22] via the shared link provided in the original paper[5]. It should be emphasized that the dataset (325,000) that we used to retrain NYAN accounts for half of original training set (650,000) and is a subset of the original training set. The datasets for molecular properties—including BBB Martins, Pgp Broccatelli and CYP2D6 Veith—were sourced from the TDCommons[23] Python package v.0.4.12. The Tox21 dataset was procured from the Tox21 database[24], as referenced in the original paper. The codes were based on Python v.3.9.13, Tensorflow v.2.10.0 and PyTorch v.2.2.2, and run on an Intel I9-13900KF processor and a NVIDIA RTX4090 GPU, with the configuration deployed on an Ubuntu 22.04 LTS system.

### Toxicology datasets

The expanded predictive toxicology dataset is sourced from TOXRIC[8]—a comprehensive and standardized toxicology database. For toxicity prediction tasks, we compiled 30 assay datasets with ~150,000 measurements related to five categories. These categories span a range of toxicity assessment, including genetic toxicity, organic toxicity, clinical toxicity, developmental and reproductive toxicity, and reactive toxicity (Supplementary Table 1). The acute toxicity dataset includes 59 endpoints with 80,081 unique compounds and 122,594 measurements, involving 15 different species such as mouse, rabbit, cat and so on; eight different administration routes such as intraperitoneal, intravenous, oral, and so on; and three different evaluation records including LD50 (lethal dose, 50%), LDLo (lethal dose low) and TDLo (toxic dose low). This dataset is very sparse since nearly 97.4% of compound-to-endpoint measurements are missing. Meanwhile, this dataset is extremely data-unbalanced with some endpoints having approximately 30,000 samples, for example, mouse-intraperitoneal-LD50 has 36,295 measurements, while others consist of only around 100 measurements such as rabbit-intraperitoneal-LD50, mouse-intravenous-LDLo and rat-intravenous-LDLo (Supplementary Fig. 2). This disparity presents acute toxicity prediction as a challenging regression problem.

### Surrogate models

The surrogate models comprise eight typical machine learning algorithms, including ExtraTree, SVM, random forest, AdaBoost, light gradient-boosting machine, gradient-boosted decision tree, XGBoost and deep forest. Especially, deep forest model is characterized by a cascade forest structure, which has fewer hyperparameters and better performance compared with the widely used deep neural networks. In this study, the deep forest contains several cascade layers with the same components and each layer consists of four ExtraTree classifiers.

### Baseline models

We have summarized four different types of popular and competitive baselines for comparison with the NYAN model in toxicity prediction. First, the transformer-based models: KPGT[11], equipped with knowledge-guided pre-training of a graph transformer; UniMol[12], which incorporates 3D information; and PharmHGT[13], which uses a heterogeneous graph transformer. Second, the GNN-based model: MolCLR[14], which performs molecular contrastive learning of representations using GNNs. Third, the LSTM-based model: BAN[15], which uses bidirectional LSTM to aggregate information from SMILES strings. Finally, the knowledge graph-based model: KANO[16], which leverages prior domain knowledge. All of these models were configured using their default settings as described in their original papers.

### Quantification and statistical analysis

The AUROC, AUPRC and accuracy are used as the evaluation metrics for toxicity classification tasks. For the regression tasks on acute toxicity datasets, the metric of determination coefficient (R²) and RMSE are used:

$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{y}_i - y_i)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\bar{y} - y_i)^2}$$

$$\mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{y}_i - y_i)^2}$$

where n denotes the total number of available compounds; y_i and ŷ_i denote the ground-truth measurement value and the predicted value for compound i, respectively; and ȳ is the averaged toxicity intensity value (−log[mol kg⁻¹]) over all compounds.

To statistically compare different molecular representation methods or surrogate methods, Friedman and post-hoc Nemenyi tests were employed. The Friedman test is a non-parametric test equivalent to the analysis of variance, and is used to ascertain whether there are statistically significant differences in the means of three or more methods tested on identical datasets. The Nemenyi test then evaluates pairwise method performances, deeming them significantly different if their rank averages exceed the critical difference, calculated as CD = q_α √(k(k + 1)/6N) (k is the number of methods, N is the number of datasets, q_α is based on Studentized range statistic divided by √2, α denotes the confidence level).

### Multi-task learning

For a fair comparison, we keep the same model architecture with previous studies[20] and adopt a three-layer fully connected neural network with a hidden size of 1,500, 500 and 100 for each respective layer, followed by a 59-way linear regressor layer. The input of this multi-task network is a 1,088-dimension feature representation of a compound that comprises a 1,024-dimension Avalon fingerprint and a 64-dimension NYAN latent. This network outputs 59 continuous values, representing the intensity of acute toxicity in terms of 59 endpoints; however, only several endpoints have been observed and annotated for each compound. The multi-task network is trained by stochastic gradient descent with Nestrerov momentum and supervised by mean-squared error loss. When making inference for a testing compound, the five random NYAN latent representations generated by a variational graph encoder for this compound are separately concatenated with their corresponding Avalon fingerprint, and fed into in parallel the well-trained multi-task network for feedforward computation. The averaged output of them is deemed as the final predicted toxicity intensity value.
